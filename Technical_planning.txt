Technical planning is the process of deciding how a software system or feature will be designed, built, tested, and deployed before development begins. It involves identifying the technical approach, resources, tools, timeline, and potential risks to ensure the project is completed successfully.

Why technical planning is important

Technical planning helps teams:

Define a clear development approach.
Reduce technical risks and uncertainties.
Estimate the time and resources required.
Ensure everyone understands the project goals.
Improve software quality and maintainability.
What is included in technical planning?

A technical plan typically covers:

Requirements analysis: Understand what the software needs to do.
System architecture: Decide how the system will be structured.
Technology stack: Choose programming languages, frameworks, databases, and tools.
Task breakdown: Divide the project into smaller, manageable tasks.
Timeline and milestones: Estimate when tasks should be completed.
Risk assessment: Identify potential technical challenges and plan how to address them.
Testing strategy: Plan how the software will be tested.
Deployment plan: Decide how the software will be released and maintained.

Benefits of technical planning
Reduces development delays.
Helps prevent costly design mistakes.
Improves collaboration among team members.
Makes project progress easier to track.
Ensures the software is scalable and maintainable.
Technical planning vs. Project planning
Technical Planning	Project Planning
Focuses on how the software will be built	Focuses on when, who, and budget
Covers architecture, tools, coding approach, testing, and deployment	Covers schedule, resources, costs, and project management
Usually led by technical leads or software architects	Usually led by project managers or product managers

Step 1 — Understand the Business

Never plan technology first.

Ask

Why are we building this?

Example

Feature:

Notifications

Question

Why?

Answer:

Increase user engagement.

Now ask

Do we need

Email?
Push?
SMS?
In-app?

Maybe only email achieves the goal.

Planning starts with understanding outcomes, not technology.

Step 2 — Gather Requirements

Separate functional from non-functional requirements.

Functional:

User uploads file
User downloads file
Delete file

Non-functional:

99.9% uptime
Upload 5 GB files
Encrypt data
Less than 200 ms latency

Many failed projects meet the functional requirements but ignore the non-functional ones.

Step 3 — Find Constraints

Everything has constraints.

Examples:

Budget

Must stay under $2,000/month.

Time

Launch in four weeks.

Traffic

10 million users.

Compliance

Must meet GDPR requirements.

Team

Two engineers available.

Constraints shape your solution more than technology choices do.

Step 4 — Break the Problem Down

Don't think:

Build video platform.

Think:

Upload

↓

Validation

↓

Storage

↓

Processing

↓

Metadata

↓

Streaming

↓

Monitoring

Each piece can now be planned independently.

Step 5 — Identify Risks

Every project has risks.

Create a table like this:

Risk	Likelihood	Impact	Mitigation
Storage costs grow too quickly	Medium	High	Compress videos, archive old content
Slow transcoding	Medium	Medium	Queue jobs, autoscale workers
Malware uploads	Low	High	Scan uploads before processing
Vendor outage	Low	High	Retries, backups, graceful degradation

Experienced planners spend significant time identifying risks early.

Step 6 — Evaluate Tradeoffs

This is where senior thinking becomes visible.

Suppose you need search.

Option A

Database LIKE query

Pros

Easy
Fast to build

Cons

Doesn't scale well

Option B

Elasticsearch

Pros

Powerful
Scalable

Cons

Operational complexity

A Staff Engineer doesn't ask:

Which is best?

They ask:

Which is best for our current needs?

Overengineering is as harmful as underengineering.

Step 7 — Think About Evolution

Imagine today

100 users

Next year

5 million users

Ask:

What changes?

Database?

Caching?

Queues?

Storage?

Indexes?

API?

You don't need to build for 5 million users today, but you should avoid choices that make growth unnecessarily difficult.

Step 9 — Deployment Strategy

Ask

Can this deploy safely?

Example

Database migration

Old application

↓

New application

↓

Migration

↓

Cleanup

Not

Migration

↓

Application crashes

Plan for compatibility between old and new versions whenever possible.

Step 10 — Operational Planning

Once deployed, ask:

How will we know it's healthy?

Plan for:

Logs

Metrics

Alerts

Tracing

Dashboards

Runbooks

Backups

Recovery

Engineers often spend more time operating systems than building them.

Think in Decisions

Every plan is a sequence of decisions.

For each one, document:

Decision

Use PostgreSQL.

Why

Strong transactional guarantees and team expertise.

Alternatives

MySQL, DynamoDB.

Tradeoffs

Slightly harder horizontal scaling, but simpler consistency model.

This makes your reasoning transparent.

Ask These Questions for Every Project
Users

Who uses it?

How many?

How often?

What's the worst thing that could happen to them?

Data

Where does it come from?

Where is it stored?

Who owns it?

Can it be lost?

How long is it retained?

Failure

What happens if:

Database fails?

Queue fails?

Cache fails?

Third-party API fails?

Network fails?

Region fails?

Design graceful degradation where possible.

Scale

What happens at

10 users?

1,000?

100,000?

10 million?

Look for bottlenecks that appear as usage grows.

Security

Who can access this?

Who should not?

Can someone modify another user's data?

What sensitive information is handled?

Maintenance

Imagine a new engineer joins in a year.

Can they understand the design from your documentation?

Can they safely make changes?

Good planning considers the next engineer as much as the current one.

The Mental Models of Staff Engineers

Instead of asking:

Can we build this?

They ask:

Should we build this?
What assumptions are we making?
What's the cheapest solution that meets today's requirements?
What decision would be hard to reverse later?
If this fails at 2 a.m., who gets paged and what will they do?
What is the blast radius if this change goes wrong?
What technical debt are we creating, and is it acceptable?
Deliberate Practice

To develop this skill, don't wait until you're assigned a design document. Practice proactively.

Take a feature from a product you know—for example:

"Users can schedule emails."
"Add dark mode."
"Support file uploads."
"Add two-factor authentication."

Before writing any code, produce a one-page technical plan covering:

The business goal
Requirements
Constraints
Proposed architecture
Alternatives
Risks
Rollout plan
Monitoring
Rollback strategy

Then ask yourself: Could another engineer build this from my plan without repeatedly asking me for clarification?

If the answer is yes, you're developing one of the core skills expected of senior and staff engineers.

A progression to Staff-level planning

A practical path looks like this:

Weeks 1–2: Learn to gather complete requirements and identify hidden assumptions.
Weeks 3–4: Practice decomposing features into components and identifying risks.
Weeks 5–6: Compare multiple architectural approaches and justify tradeoffs.
Weeks 7–8: Add rollout, observability, rollback, and operational planning.
Weeks 9–10: Present your design to others, invite criticism, and revise it based on feedback.

One of the best ways to learn is through repetition. 
Give yourself a real feature, write a design document, and have someone challenge every assumption. Over time, you'll stop thinking in terms of code and start thinking in terms of systems, risk, and long-term engineering decisions—the hallmark of a senior technical planner.
